# Domestic Structural and Institutional Flaws

While geopolitical factors establish the external conditions enabling terrorism, the persistence and adaptability of militant organizations in Pakistan cannot be fully explained without examining domestic institutional weaknesses. This section analyzes how governance deficits, justice sector failures, and incomplete administrative integration have created permissive environments that non-state armed groups exploit. These domestic preconditions interact with external precipitants to produce the sustained violence patterns observed since 2021.

## 1.6 Weak Governance and Policy Deficits

### 1.6.1 Political Instability and NAP Failure

Pakistan's counter-terrorism efforts have been fundamentally undermined by recurring political instability. Between 2014 and 2024, the country experienced four changes in government, each accompanied by significant policy discontinuities in security affairs. This pattern of political churning has prevented the development of sustained, coherent counter-terrorism strategies capable of addressing deeply embedded militant networks.

The National Action Plan (NAP), adopted in January 2015 following a major school attack in Peshawar that resulted in significant casualties, represents Pakistan's most comprehensive attempt at coordinated counter-terrorism policy. The twenty-point framework encompassed military operations against militant sanctuaries, judicial reforms, countering hate speech and extremism, choking terror financing, registration of madrassas, and addressing the FATA governance vacuum. NAP emerged from rare consensus among political parties and the security establishment, raising expectations for transformative change.

Implementation assessment reveals a mixed but largely disappointing record. Of the twenty NAP points, security analysts and civil society organizations assess that only five to seven achieved meaningful implementation by 2024. Military operations in North Waziristan and subsequent areas (Operation Zarb-e-Azb and Operation Radd-ul-Fasaad) succeeded in dismantling territorial control of militant groups---a genuine achievement that reduced violence from 2015 to 2020. The establishment of military courts, though controversial, processed hundreds of terrorism cases. These successes demonstrate that focused implementation can yield results.

However, the majority of NAP commitments saw partial or negligible implementation. Madrassa registration and curriculum reform proceeded haltingly, with estimates suggesting only 30 to 40 percent of seminaries completed registration by 2023. Counter-financing measures struggled against deeply embedded informal networks. The commitment to counter extremist narratives and hate speech produced few concrete outcomes. Most critically, the promise to address FATA's governance vacuum remained largely unfulfilled for years after the merger.

Policy discontinuity manifested in concrete ways. Each administration adjusted counter-terrorism priorities according to its own assessment and political calculations. The transfer of registered First Information Reports between civilian and military court jurisdictions created procedural confusion. Provincial governments in Sindh and Punjab diverged from federal priorities, with coordination mechanisms remaining weak. The National Counter Terrorism Authority (NACTA), designed as the institutional anchor for policy coherence, suffered from inadequate staffing, unclear mandate, and political marginalization across multiple governments.

Federal-provincial coordination failures proved particularly damaging. Counter-terrorism is a concurrent subject under Pakistan's constitutional framework, requiring close collaboration between federal security agencies and provincial police and administration. In practice, intelligence sharing remained incomplete, jurisdictional disputes delayed operations, and resource allocation between tiers of government remained contested. The Merged Districts suffered most acutely from this coordination deficit, falling between federal security prerogatives and delayed provincial administrative extension.

## 1.7 Institutional Weakness in the Justice Sector

### 1.7.1 Anti-Terrorism Courts (ATCs) Challenges

Pakistan's Anti-Terrorism Courts, established under the Anti-Terrorism Act of 1997 and subsequently amended, constitute the specialized judicial mechanism for prosecuting terrorism-related offenses. The ATC system operates across all provinces and federal territories, with designated judges empowered to conduct expedited trials and impose enhanced sentences. In principle, ATCs should serve as the critical judicial link between security operations and sustainable deterrence through criminal accountability.

In practice, ATC performance has been marked by significant institutional weaknesses. Conviction rates, while difficult to assess precisely due to inconsistent data reporting, are estimated at 15 to 25 percent for terrorism-related cases---substantially lower than conviction rates for ordinary criminal offenses. Annual variations show some improvement following specific reform efforts, but structural problems persist. High acquittal rates undermine deterrence, waste investigative resources, and allow militants to return to operational activities.

Witness protection inadequacies constitute perhaps the most critical failure point. Pakistan lacks a comprehensive witness protection program for terrorism cases. Witnesses, their families, and sometimes legal professionals face intimidation, threats, and in documented cases, physical harm. The absence of protected facilities, identity protection mechanisms, and relocation programs means that eyewitness testimony---often crucial in terrorism prosecutions---is frequently unavailable or recanted under pressure. This gap alone explains a significant proportion of acquittals.

Investigation standards represent another systemic deficiency. Evidence collection in terrorism cases often occurs in conflict-affected areas under challenging conditions. Chain of custody documentation remains inconsistent, rendering forensic evidence vulnerable to defense challenges. Forensic laboratory capacity, while improving, remains inadequate for the caseload. Digital evidence extraction and analysis capabilities lag behind the technological sophistication of militant communications. Investigating officers frequently lack specialized training in terrorism case development, and transfers disrupt case continuity.

Prosecution quality issues compound investigative weaknesses. Prosecutorial services suffer from inadequate specialization, heavy caseloads, and limited coordination with investigating agencies. Case files often reach prosecutors with gaps that prove fatal at trial. The adversarial nature of criminal proceedings means that well-resourced defense challenges can exploit procedural weaknesses that might be overlooked in less scrutinized cases. Some assessments suggest that prosecutorial turnover and inadequate preparation contribute as significantly to acquittals as investigative failures.

These judicial sector weaknesses create a systemic accountability gap. Security forces conduct operations and detain suspects, but the judicial pathway to conviction and incarceration functions poorly. This pattern generates multiple negative consequences: militants learn that arrest does not necessarily mean punishment; security forces may resort to extra-judicial measures when judicial processes appear futile; and the broader public loses confidence in the state's capacity to deliver justice through legal channels.

## 1.8 Failure of Integration in Merged Districts

The 2018 Twenty-Fifth Constitutional Amendment merged the Federally Administered Tribal Areas (FATA) into Khyber Pakhtunkhwa province, formally ending the colonial-era Frontier Crimes Regulation (FCR) that had governed the region since 1901. This constitutional transformation represented a historic opportunity to extend full citizenship rights, civilian governance, and access to justice to approximately five million residents of the newly designated Merged Districts. The promise was profound: replacing a framework of collective punishment and political agent authority with representative government and judicial access.

Implementation, however, created a protracted governance vacuum that militant organizations exploited. The merger envisioned a transition period during which provincial administration, police, and judiciary would extend into the Merged Districts. In practice, this extension proceeded far more slowly than anticipated. Provincial administrative officers were slow to deploy; many positions remained vacant years after merger. Resource allocation for the Merged Districts, while increased on paper, faced bureaucratic delays and competing provincial priorities.

The delay in establishing civilian judicial control proved particularly consequential. The FCR's abolition created a legal vacuum before adequate court infrastructure was established. By 2022, only a fraction of the promised courts were operational in the Merged Districts. Residents seeking civil dispute resolution or criminal justice faced journeys to distant courts in settled areas of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa. This access-to-justice gap meant that informal dispute resolution mechanisms, including those potentially influenced by militant actors, filled the void.

The TTP exploited these governance gaps systematically. As Pakistani security forces reduced their presence following the 2020-2021 ceasefire negotiations and the Afghan Taliban's assumption of power across the border, TTP elements returned to former areas of influence. In the absence of robust civilian administration, police presence, and judicial access, militant actors provided alternative governance functions---dispute resolution, social services, and security in exchange for support or acquiescence. This pattern represents classic insurgent exploitation of governance vacuums documented across conflict contexts.

Security force transition challenges compounded administrative failures. The merger envisioned a gradual shift from Frontier Corps (FC) dominance to provincial police primacy. The FC, a federal paramilitary force with deep local knowledge and established presence, was to cede ground to KP Police, which required massive expansion, training, and equipment to operate effectively in the Merged Districts. This transition remains incomplete. Police force strength in the Merged Districts remains below authorized levels. Training for operating in a post-conflict environment with active militant presence has been inadequate. Intelligence-sharing between FC and police has faced institutional friction. The result is a security force posture that is neither the FC's established counterinsurgency presence nor a mature police service capable of maintaining civilian law and order.

The domestic institutional weaknesses examined in this section---governance discontinuity, justice sector failures, and incomplete integration of the Merged Districts---interact with the geopolitical preconditions discussed previously to create a compound vulnerability. External sanctuaries and regional instability provide operational depth for militant organizations, while domestic gaps provide the space for recruitment, resource mobilization, and local consolidation. The following section examines how socio-economic marginalization and ideological narratives further fuel the recruitment and radicalization processes that sustain these organizations.

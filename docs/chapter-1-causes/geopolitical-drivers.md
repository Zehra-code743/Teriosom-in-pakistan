# Geopolitical and Regional Drivers

The preceding section established that Pakistan's terrorism challenge cannot be adequately explained through monocausal frameworks. This section examines the geopolitical dimension---the regional dynamics and historical legacies that constitute essential preconditions for contemporary violence. While domestic factors addressed in subsequent sections determine how these external pressures manifest internally, the geopolitical context shapes the opportunity structures within which non-state armed groups operate.

## 1.3 The Historical Continuum of Conflict

### 1.3.1 The Soviet War Legacy (1979-1989)

The Soviet intervention in Afghanistan in December 1979 initiated transformations in the region whose consequences persist into the present. Understanding these historical dynamics is essential for analyzing contemporary terrorism in Pakistan, as many current structural vulnerabilities trace their origins to decisions made during this period.

**State Sponsorship of Proxy Forces**

The decade-long conflict against Soviet forces in Afghanistan involved substantial external support channeled through Pakistan. The collaboration between the United States, Saudi Arabia, and Pakistani intelligence services to arm and train resistance fighters has been extensively documented in scholarly literature by researchers including Steve Coll and Ahmed Rashid. This arrangement created institutional knowledge, networks, and infrastructure for supporting non-state armed actors---capabilities that outlasted their original Cold War purpose.

The long-term consequences of this proxy arrangement proved significant. Infrastructure established for supporting anti-Soviet operations---training facilities, logistical networks, funding channels---did not simply disappear following the Soviet withdrawal in 1989. As multiple scholars have observed, these capabilities proved adaptable to subsequent conflicts, contributing to what scholars term "blowback" effects that complicated Pakistan's security environment in subsequent decades.

**The Kalashnikov Culture**

The massive influx of weapons during the 1980s fundamentally altered the security landscape of western Pakistan. Estimates suggest millions of small arms entered the region during this period, with substantial quantities remaining in circulation after the conflict's conclusion. This phenomenon, frequently termed "Kalashnikov Culture" in academic literature, normalized weapons possession and use in ways that persisted long after the original strategic rationale had passed.

The weapons proliferation extended beyond mere availability to encompass broader social transformations. Communities in the tribal areas and adjacent regions developed what sociologists describe as militarized social structures, where weapons ownership became intertwined with concepts of masculine honor and community defense. These cultural adaptations created environments where subsequent militant recruitment efforts encountered fewer social barriers.

**Foreign Fighter Influx and Militant Infrastructure**

The Afghan conflict attracted fighters from across the Muslim world, many of whom received training in facilities located in Pakistan's tribal areas---regions that would later become the Merged Districts following the 2018 constitutional amendment. While the majority eventually departed, a meaningful number remained, either settling permanently or maintaining networks that could be reactivated for subsequent conflicts.

The infrastructure established in the Federally Administered Tribal Areas (FATA) during this period proved particularly consequential. Training facilities, weapons caches, and organizational networks created to support anti-Soviet operations provided foundations that subsequent militant organizations would later exploit. The institutional weakness of state authority in these areas---a precondition discussed further in Section III---meant that this infrastructure operated with minimal governmental oversight or control.

## 1.4 Instability in Afghanistan (Post-2021)

### 1.4.1 TTP Sanctuary and Strategic Depth

The Afghan Taliban's assumption of power in August 2021 constitutes what the preconditions-precipitants framework identifies as a major precipitating event. The political transformation in Kabul fundamentally altered the operational environment for the Tehrik-i-Taliban Pakistan (TTP), enabling a resurgence that Pakistani security analysts had warned of during the preceding years.

**Evidence of Sanctuary Conditions**

Multiple authoritative sources document the sanctuary conditions the TTP enjoys under Afghan Taliban governance. United Nations Security Council Monitoring Team reports consistently identify TTP leadership presence in Afghanistan and assess that the organization operates with significant freedom within Afghan territory. The International Crisis Group and other analytical organizations have similarly documented the TTP's capacity to plan, coordinate, and launch operations from Afghan territory.

The Afghan Taliban's position on the TTP reflects what scholars describe as organizational dilemmas created by historical relationships. Public statements from Kabul officials emphasizing non-interference in Pakistani affairs contrast with the documented operational reality. Academic analysts offer varying interpretations: some emphasize ideological solidarity between the movements, while others highlight the Afghan Taliban's limited capacity to control all armed groups within its territory.

**Doha Accord Implications**

The February 2020 Doha Agreement between the United States and the Afghan Taliban included provisions regarding the use of Afghan territory for attacks against other nations. The subsequent escalation of TTP operations from Afghan territory raises questions about compliance with these commitments that scholars continue to debate.

Pakistani officials and some Western analysts characterize the situation as a breach of Doha commitments, while others note the agreement's ambiguities regarding which groups fall under its provisions. This analytical disagreement reflects broader uncertainties about the Afghan Taliban's willingness and capacity to constrain the TTP's operations against Pakistan.

### 1.4.2 Failure of Border Management

**Structural Weaknesses**

The approximately 2,600-kilometer frontier between Pakistan and Afghanistan presents formidable border management challenges. Pakistan has invested substantially in fencing infrastructure since 2017, yet structural limitations constrain effectiveness. The terrain encompasses high-altitude mountain passes, remote valleys, and areas where the international boundary remains physically ambiguous on the ground.

The border fencing project, while representing significant investment, addresses only a portion of the management challenge. Scholarly assessments note that physical infrastructure cannot substitute for the intelligence capabilities, community cooperation, and administrative capacity required for effective border security. The Merged Districts' integration challenges, discussed in Section III, compound these structural difficulties.

**Monitoring and Infiltration Challenges**

Security analysts identify persistent gaps in border monitoring capacity. While detailed discussion of infiltration patterns falls outside this analysis's appropriate scope, the scholarly consensus acknowledges that the border remains permeable to determined actors despite substantial security investments. Geographic constraints, resource limitations, and the complexity of managing a frontier bisecting communities with familial and ethnic ties on both sides create enduring vulnerabilities.

## 1.5 External State Rivalries and Proxies

Regional rivalries and their potential connection to non-state armed groups represent the most analytically contested dimension of Pakistan's terrorism challenge. This section presents multiple scholarly perspectives without endorsing any particular attribution.

**Historical Patterns**

Scholars of South Asian security generally acknowledge that the region has witnessed patterns of state support for non-state actors across multiple dyadic relationships. This historical pattern---documented in varying degrees for multiple regional actors---created what international relations scholars term a "proxy equilibrium" that contributed to regional instability. The academic literature debates the extent, continuity, and current status of such relationships.

**Contested Contemporary Claims**

Pakistani official sources and some analysts allege that external actors provide support to organizations including the BLA operating in Balochistan. According to these perspectives, regional rivals exploit ethnic grievances and separatist sentiments to destabilize Pakistan. Pakistani officials have presented what they characterize as evidence of external involvement in specific incidents.

However, scholarly assessments of these claims vary considerably. Some Western analysts express skepticism about the extent of alleged external involvement, attributing Balochistan's instability primarily to domestic governance failures and legitimate grievances inadequately addressed by the Pakistani state. Afghan sources deny allegations of support for anti-Pakistan groups, attributing border tensions to Pakistani policy decisions.

Indian scholarly perspectives generally reject allegations of involvement in Pakistani terrorism, characterizing such claims as diversionary tactics that avoid addressing domestic drivers of extremism. Pakistani academic and official sources counter that documented evidence supports their allegations, though they acknowledge that conclusive public attribution remains challenging given the covert nature of alleged support.

**Analytical Limitations**

The contested nature of attribution claims reflects genuine evidentiary challenges. Intelligence assessments rarely become publicly available in forms permitting independent scholarly verification. Political incentives exist for all regional actors to make or deny attribution claims regardless of underlying facts. Responsible scholarship requires acknowledging these limitations while presenting the range of interpretations that informed analysts advance.

What the scholarly record does support is that Pakistan's terrorism challenge cannot be adequately understood without reference to regional dynamics, whether those dynamics involve active external exploitation of internal vulnerabilities or the more indirect effects of regional instability and conflict spillover. The relative weight of external versus domestic factors remains a matter of ongoing analytical debate.

---

The geopolitical factors examined in this section---historical legacies of the Soviet-Afghan War, post-2021 Afghan instability, border management challenges, and regional rivalries---constitute essential elements of the preconditions enabling terrorism in Pakistan. However, these external factors interact with domestic institutional weaknesses and governance failures that determine how geopolitical pressures manifest within Pakistani society. Section III turns to these domestic institutional dimensions, examining how governance deficits, policy failures, and administrative capacity gaps compound the challenges created by Pakistan's geopolitical environment.
